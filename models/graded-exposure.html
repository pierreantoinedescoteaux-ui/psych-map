Model

Graded exposure

A ladder from the mild version of a dreaded thing to the real one, climbed one boring rung at a time.

Use

Useful to shrink a dread by approaching it in steps you can actually take.

How to use it

  1. 01
    Name the thing you keep avoiding and write five to eight versions of it, ordered from mildest to the real thing. For a dreaded conversation: write out what you'd say, say it alone out loud, raise the topic lightly with the person, have the short version, have the whole thing.
  2. 02
    Start on a rung that raises your pulse but stays doable — around a four out of ten. A rung that costs nothing teaches nothing, and a rung that overwhelms you just confirms the alarm.
  3. 03
    Stay with the rung until the discomfort noticeably falls, rather than exiting at the peak. Leaving at the peak files the experience as an escape; leaving after the drop files it as evidence.
  4. 04
    Repeat the same rung until it's merely tedious. Boredom is the graduation signal; then take the next rung up.
  5. 05
    When a rung keeps beating you, don't abandon the ladder — split the gap and build an in-between rung. The ladder was a guess, so revise it like one.

Graded exposure shrinks a dread by approaching it in ordered steps: you build a ladder from a mild version of the thing to the full version, start low, and climb only when the current rung stops being scary. The mechanism runs on what avoidance does. Every time you dodge the difficult email or the overdue conversation, you get relief, and the relief teaches your alarm system that dodging saved you — so the alarm stays calibrated to a threat it never gets to test. Exposure reverses the lesson by supplying the missing data. You stay in contact with a manageable dose of the thing until the discomfort crests and drops on its own, which it does, and the alarm recalibrates from experience rather than argument. The graded part matters as much as the exposure part: steps sized to be survivable are what make the evidence collectible.

Where it comes fromShow less

In depth

The ladder descends from Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitization, published in 1958: clients climbed an imagined hierarchy of feared scenes while held in deep muscle relaxation, on the theory that a relaxed body inhibits a fear response. The hierarchy survived better than the theory. Later research found the relaxation was largely optional and that real-world contact outperformed imagination, leaving the working core we still use: ordered steps and repeated contact, held until the fear subsides. Underneath sits the behaviorist reading of avoidance: relief is a reward, dodging is a behavior, and rewarded behavior grows. The everyday version applies the same mechanics to inboxes and conversations, no couch required.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    Marcus handles everything by email. Phone calls get screened, voicemails accumulate, and last month he drove forty minutes to a supplier to avoid a five-minute call. Each workaround brings relief, and each relief deepens the groove. His ladder starts absurdly small: day one, listen to the backlog of voicemails without responding. Then a call where no human answers — the pharmacy's refill line. Then a scripted question to a colleague who likes him. Then the easy vendor. Two weeks of rungs, each repeated until dull, before the one that started all this: the client whose invoice dispute has been sitting for a month. By then the call is unpleasant rather than impossible, which was the entire point of the ladder. Nothing about the client changed. The alarm did.

  • Vividly

    The email has been bold in the inbox for nine days. It has developed gravity; the whole inbox now orbits it, and checking mail means a sideways glance with unfocused eyes, the way you'd pass a dog you don't trust. The ladder treats the monster as a set of parts. Rung one: open the message, read it once, close it — no reply owed. The pulse spikes, hangs, and then, with nothing to feed it, sags. Rung two, next day: read it and jot what it's actually asking, which turns out to be two things, one of them easy. Rung three: draft without sending. By the time the reply goes out, the email has shrunk back into a rectangle of text. It was the dodging, all along, that kept it fed.
