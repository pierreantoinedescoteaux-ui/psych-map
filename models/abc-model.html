Model

The ABC model

A three-step frame for catching the belief that sits between an event and how you end up feeling.

Use

Useful to see the link between an event, the belief you add to it, and the consequence, and to rewire the belief.

How to use it

  1. 01
    Catch a moment where your mood turned. Write the activating event in one sentence, the way a camera would record it — no interpretation, no guesses about anyone's intent.
  2. 02

    Write the belief

    the sentence you told yourself about what the event meant. If several come up, pick the one with the most heat.
  3. 03
    Name the consequence — the feeling, and what you did or avoided because of it. This shows you what the belief is costing.
  4. 04
    Dispute the belief like a friendly skeptic. What is the actual evidence for it? What else could the event mean?
  5. 05
    Write the belief you'd offer a friend in the same situation, and notice what the feeling does. It usually drops a size.

The ABC model splits an emotional reaction into three parts: the Activating event (what happened), the Belief (what you told yourself it meant), and the Consequence (what you felt and did next). Albert Ellis's point was that A does not produce C directly. The belief in the middle does the work, which is good news, because the belief is the one part you can examine. Two people sit through the same delayed train; one arrives annoyed, the other arrives composing a resignation letter. Same A, different B. The model doesn't ask you to feel differently by force. It asks you to find the sentence you added to the event, write it down, and check whether it survives contact with the evidence. Most of the time the event was smaller than the belief made it.

Where it comes fromShow less

In depth

Albert Ellis introduced the model in the mid-1950s as the core of rational emotive behavior therapy, then gave it full form in Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy (1962). This predates Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy by roughly a decade; the two approaches converged on the same insight — reactions run through appraisals — and the ABC frame is now taught across the whole cognitive-behavioral family. Ellis later extended the letters: D for disputing the belief, E for the new effect that follows. His own disputation style was famously blunt. The everyday version doesn't need the bluntness, just the habit of asking a belief to show its evidence.

Explain it differently

  • Plainly

    A friend walks past you in the grocery store without saying hi. That is the entire event: a person walked by. The belief arrives instantly — "she saw me and decided not to stop, I must have done something" — and by the frozen aisle you're replaying your last three conversations. The consequence is a ruined errand and a stiff text that evening. Run it through the model and the event shrinks back to its actual size: someone walked past a person she may not have seen, in a store, while thinking about dinner. The misery was manufactured between A and C, and the manufacturing step is the one place you had options.

  • At work

    An email from your manager lands at 4:55: "Let's talk tomorrow." Nothing else. By dinner you've inventoried every mistake of the past quarter; by midnight you're rehearsing your defense. The activating event was four words and a time. Everything else — the audit, the trial, the verdict — came from the belief "this means trouble." Tomorrow it turns out to be about the offsite. The model doesn't claim short emails are never bad news. It says you should notice how much story you built on how little text, because your body responds to the story, not the email.

  • In a relationship

    Sam's partner is quiet at dinner. One-word answers, no follow-up questions. Sam's belief arrives fully formed: "I did something, and this is the freeze-out." He spends the meal scanning the last two days for the offense, then gets quietly defensive about a crime nobody has named. The model would have him separate the parts: the event is a tired person eating; the belief is "silence means I'm in trouble"; the consequence is defensiveness that turns a quiet dinner into an actual cold one. Asked directly, his partner says the day was long and the quiet wasn't about him. The belief made it about him.
