Model

The premortem

Before the plan starts, assume it has already failed and have everyone write down why.

Use

Useful to find a plan's weak points before launch by assuming it already failed.

How to use it

  1. 01
    Run it when the plan is drafted but not yet committed, with the people who would actually execute it. Open with the frame stated as fact: it's a year from now, the plan went ahead, and it failed badly.
  2. 02
    Give everyone two or three minutes to write reasons for the failure, silently and independently. The silence matters — it collects the doubts of people who don't argue with confident rooms.
  3. 03
    Go around the table, one reason per person per round, until the reasons run out. Record everything and defend nothing; the ban on rebuttal is what keeps the list honest.
  4. 04

    Sort the list three ways

    what the plan should change now, what needs a tripwire to watch for, and what you'll knowingly accept. A premortem that changes nothing was theater.
  5. 05
    Keep the list next to the plan and reread it at the midpoint, when the original optimism has grown back.

A premortem reverses the postmortem: before the plan starts, you assume it has already failed, thoroughly, and work out why. Gary Klein described the exercise in 2007 as a fix for a specific moment in project life — the kickoff, when confidence peaks and dissent is at its most expensive. The mechanics borrow a finding about what researchers call prospective hindsight. Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington showed in 1989 that treating an outcome as certain, rather than merely possible, makes people markedly better at generating reasons for it. "What could go wrong?" produces polite shrugs; "it went wrong — what happened?" produces a list. The exercise also solves a social problem. In a room full of committed people, voicing doubt reads as disloyalty. The premortem makes doubt the assignment, so the person holding the sharpest objection finally has a safe way to hand it over.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    The database migration plan had survived four review meetings. At the premortem, the room is told the migration failed, catastrophically, and asked to explain. The project lead writes about timeline slippage. The director writes about competing priorities. Ana, the quietest engineer on the team, writes: "We assumed the vendor's export tool works at our scale. Nobody has run it on more than a sample." In four review meetings, that sentence never surfaced — review meetings ask "any concerns?", and Ana doesn't fight confident rooms. The premortem didn't ask her to fight anyone. It asked her to complete an assignment, in writing, at the same time as everyone else. The export tool gets tested the following week. It chokes at a tenth of production volume.

  • Devil's advocate

    The exercise fails in two reliable ways. The first is theater: the team generates its list, the facilitator says "great inputs," the list goes into a slide appendix, and the plan sails on unchanged — worse than skipping the exercise, because now the project carries a talisman. "We did a premortem" becomes the reason not to worry. The second failure is quieter. In some rooms the truest answer is "the director will rewrite the requirements in month two, the way he does every quarter," and nobody writes down a sentence with a name in it. The list fills with safe entries instead, scope creep and "unforeseen dependencies." A premortem can only surface what people can afford to say. Run in a guarded room, it mostly measures the guardedness, while stamping the plan as stress-tested.

Counters

  • →Counterseffect

    Planning fallacy

    Declaring the project already failed unlocks the obstacle list that best-case forecasting edits out of the schedule.

  • →Counterseffect

    Optimism bias

    Assuming the plan has already failed and explaining why forces the search for risks that optimism quietly skips.

  • →Counterseffect

    Semmelweis reflex

    A premortem makes contrary evidence a sanctioned deliverable — 'assume our belief failed; what killed it?' — instead of an attack to be repelled.
