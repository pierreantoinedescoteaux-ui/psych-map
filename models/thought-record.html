Model

The thought record

A worksheet that puts a hot thought on trial: evidence for, evidence against, and a fairer verdict.

Use

Useful to get a stressful thought on paper and test it against the evidence.

How to use it

  1. 01
    When a mood spikes, write the situation in facts: where you were, what happened, who said what. No interpretation yet.
  2. 02
    Name the moods in single words — anxious, ashamed, angry — and rate each 0 to 100.
  3. 03
    Write the thoughts that were going through your head, and circle the hot one: the thought that moves the rating most when you say it.
  4. 04
    List the evidence for the hot thought. Facts only — things a stranger reviewing the case would accept, not feelings restated as proof.
  5. 05
    List the evidence against, held to the same standard. Include the boring exculpatory facts the thought skipped: the deadline you did hit, the review that was fine.
  6. 06
    Write a balanced thought that accounts for both columns, then re-rate the mood. Look for movement, not a transplant — anxious 80 down to anxious 45 is the tool working.

A thought record is a worksheet that puts a distressing thought on trial. In its common seven-column form you log the situation, the mood and its intensity, the automatic thought that spiked it, the evidence for that thought, the evidence against, a balanced alternative, and the mood re-rated. The format comes out of Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, where writing thoughts down and testing them was the core homework; Dennis Greenberger and Christine Padesky's Mind Over Mood standardized the version most people use. The trial framing matters. A thought record is not positive thinking — the goal is accuracy, not cheerfulness, and sometimes the evidence column confirms the thought and hands you a real problem to solve instead of a mood to manage. Hot thoughts feel true in direct proportion to how bad they feel. Paper is where that correlation goes to be checked.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    I sent a draft to my editor and got back one word: "Fine." Hot thought, verbatim: "She hated it and is deciding how to fire me." Belief rating: 90 percent. The evidence-for column was thin once feelings weren't admissible — one short email, sent at 6:40 p.m. The against column filled up embarrassingly fast: she has flagged real problems bluntly before, the last two pieces ran without cuts, she answers curtly whenever her kids are home. Balanced thought: "Fine means fine until proven otherwise; short is her default, not a verdict." Belief in the original: down to 25. The piece ran the next week, untouched. I keep doing the records because I'll believe the next "Fine" too.

  • In a relationship

    Priya's friend cancels dinner twice in a month, and the thought lands with a thud: "She's done with me and hasn't said so." A record forces the case into columns. For: two cancellations, shorter texts than usual. Against: the friend initiated the second reschedule, sent a long voice note last week, started a job Priya knows is eating her alive, and has cancelled during every crunch of the past decade. Balanced thought: "She's underwater, and this is what her underwater looks like; if the pattern holds through summer, I can ask her directly." The mood drops from a churn to an ordinary missing-someone. What changes isn't the friendship. It's whether Priya spends March prosecuting it.

On this map

Counters

  • →Counterseffect

    Confirmation bias

    The evidence-against column forces a search for disconfirming facts the hot thought would never volunteer.

  • →Counterseffect

    Imposter syndrome

    The fraud feeling is a claim about evidence, and the record's for-and-against columns put the accomplishments that got filed as luck back into the case.

  • →Counterseffect

    Motivated reasoning

    Writing evidence for and against in separate columns forces the balanced search that a wanted conclusion quietly skips.
