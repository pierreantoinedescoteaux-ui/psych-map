Model

WOOP

Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan: imagine the goal achieved, then face the thing in you that keeps stopping it.

Use

Useful to turn a wish into a plan that survives its own obstacles.

How to use it

  1. 01

    Wish

    pick one wish for the coming weeks that is challenging but genuinely feasible, and put it in a short phrase. "Run the 10k in May," not "get fit."
  2. 02

    Outcome

    name the best thing about the wish coming true, then close your eyes and imagine it fully for a minute or so. Let it be enjoyable; the pull is a working part.
  3. 03

    Obstacle

    find the main obstacle in you, the evening inertia or the dread of a clumsy first draft, and imagine it just as fully. If the obstacle you found is another person or the weather, look again.
  4. 04

    Plan

    write one sentence, "If [the obstacle shows up], then I will [specific action]." The whole sequence takes about five minutes, and it bears repeating tomorrow with the same wish.

WOOP walks a goal through four moves: name the Wish, imagine the Outcome, find the Obstacle, make a Plan. The sequence comes out of Gabriele Oettingen's research on positive fantasy, which turned up an uncomfortable result: vividly imagining a wished-for future, on its own, tends to reduce the effort people then invest. The imagining feels like progress, and the mind relaxes a little, as if the thing were partly done. Oettingen's correction, which she calls mental contrasting, keeps the fantasy but makes it face reality. Right after savoring the outcome, you locate the main obstacle — specifically the one inside you, the habit or feeling that actually stops you, since circumstances rarely explain a wish that is feasible and still unmet. The final move ties an if-then plan to that exact obstacle. The order is the technique: outcome before obstacle gives the wish its pull, and obstacle after outcome tells the pull where the wall is.

In depth

Oettingen spent two decades documenting the fantasy effect before packaging the remedy. Across studies of students and job seekers, the pattern repeated: the rosier the private imagery of success, the weaker the follow-through, as if the fantasy paid out a small advance on a reward not yet earned. Mental contrasting emerged as the fix, and pairing it with Peter Gollwitzer's if-then planning produced the full method — MCII in the academic literature, WOOP in her 2014 book for general readers. One boundary condition matters: contrasting energizes feasible wishes and deflates infeasible ones. Oettingen counts the deflation as a feature, since a wish you quietly drop frees the effort for one you can actually reach.

Explain it differently

  • First person

    My wish was to finally submit stories to magazines — a thing I'd "been doing" for two years without one submission. The outcome step was easy and embarrassing: I'd imagined the acceptance email so many times I could recite it. The obstacle step was the surprise. I assumed my obstacle was time. It wasn't. Sitting with it, the real sequence surfaced: I open the file, reread the first page, cringe, and close the laptop with a feeling I'd been calling "busy." The obstacle was the cringe. My plan: if I open the file and feel it, then I write for ten minutes before I'm allowed to judge a word. Two stories went out that month. The fantasy hadn't been fuel after all. It had been the exhaust.

  • In a relationship

    Priya's wish is real evenings with her partner, the kind they had before both jobs got serious. The outcome she pictures is specific: the long unhurried dinner where the conversation wanders somewhere neither of them planned. Then the obstacle step, and the honest answer is unflattering — it isn't his schedule, which she'd been blaming. It's her own hand reaching for the work phone within ten minutes of clearing the plates, "just to check," after which the evening quietly becomes two people in the same room administrating separate worlds. Her plan writes itself: if my hand goes for the phone after dinner, then the phone goes in the hallway drawer until morning. The wish, it turns out, was hers to grant all along.
