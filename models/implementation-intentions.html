Model

Implementation intentions

An if-then sentence that decides in advance when, where, and how you'll act, so the moment doesn't get to renegotiate.

Use

Useful to make follow-through automatic by deciding the when and the what in advance.

How to use it

  1. 01
    Pick one intention that keeps not happening — "reply to invoices," "stretch," "call my grandmother." One, not five.
  2. 02
    Choose a cue that reliably occurs on its own: a time and place ("when I sit down at my desk at 9") or an event ("when I pour the first coffee"). You have to be able to notice it without trying.
  3. 03

    Write the full sentence

    "If [cue], then I will [action]." Make the action small enough to finish in one go.
  4. 04
    Say it to yourself once with intent, then leave it alone. The technique is deciding once, in advance — not repeating an affirmation.
  5. 05
    If it hasn't fired within a week, change the cue, not the goal. A cue that needs remembering was never a cue.

An implementation intention is a plan written as one conditional sentence: "If situation X arises, then I will do Y." It differs from a goal intention ("I will exercise more") by deciding in advance exactly when, where, and how the action happens. Peter Gollwitzer, who named the format in 1999, found that the sentence does something a goal alone doesn't: it hands the job of starting the action to the cue. When the if-part shows up, the then-part fires with much less deliberation, which means less room for the present moment to renegotiate. A meta-analysis of nearly a hundred studies put the effect at medium to large — unusual for a technique that takes under a minute. The catch is that the sentence only works for goals you actually hold. It's an ignition system, not an engine.

Explain it differently

  • First person

    I meant to take my vitamin D all winter. The bottle sat in the cupboard; the goal sat in my head; neither moved. The fix took ten seconds: "If I put the kettle on in the morning, then I take the vitamin while it heats." I put the kettle on every single morning without deciding to, and now the pill rides along. What surprised me is how little it feels like discipline. There's no moment of choosing anymore — the kettle chooses. Six weeks in, I've missed twice, both while traveling, when the kettle wasn't there. Which proved the point: the cue was doing all the work.

  • At work

    Meetings at Dana's company end with a chorus of "I'll follow up on that," and about a third of the follow-ups ever happen. Dana's version stopped leaking when she made one rule: "If a meeting ends with something on my plate, then I write the follow-up message before I open anything else." Not "I'll be better about follow-ups" — a specific action bolted to a specific moment. The meeting ending is the cue, and it comes to her; she doesn't have to remember anything at some better time that never arrives. Colleagues assume she has a system. She has a sentence.

  • Devil's advocate

    The format fails quietly when the goal underneath is ambivalent. "If it's Saturday morning, then I will work on the novel" does nothing if part of you has stopped wanting to write the novel — the cue fires, you watch it fire, you make toast. If-then planning automates initiation; it can't settle an argument you're having with yourself, and using it to steamroll that argument just adds guilt on top of avoidance. It also doesn't stack: people who write out fifteen if-then rules discover they've built a small legal code nobody consults. One or two live plans beat a policy manual. When a plan keeps failing, the interesting question is rarely the cue.

On this map

Counters

  • →Counterseffect

    Present bias

    The if-then format commits the action to a cue in advance, so the in-the-moment preference for comfort never gets a vote.

  • →Counterseffect

    Ego depletion

    Deciding in advance removes the need for in-the-moment restraint, which works regardless of whether willpower is a resource.

  • →Counterseffect

    Empathy gap

    An if-then plan written in a cold state fires on cue in the hot one, so the moment of temptation never has to relitigate the decision.

  • →Counterseffect

    Moral licensing

    Licensing operates at the moment a choice gets renegotiated; a pre-decided if-then plan removes that moment, so past virtue never gets to argue for the exception.

  • →Counterseffect

    Zeigarnik effect

    Committing to a specific plan for the unfinished task has been shown to quiet its intrusions even before any of the work gets done.
