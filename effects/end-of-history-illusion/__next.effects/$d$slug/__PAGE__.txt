6:T5e6,<p>One likely engine is a fluency shortcut. Remembering how you changed is easy; the evidence is on file. Imagining a specific future self with different convictions is hard, and the difficulty gets misread as improbability — &quot;I can&#39;t picture wanting something else&quot; becomes &quot;I won&#39;t.&quot; There may be a motivated layer too: believing you have finally arrived at yourself is a comfortable place to stop.</p>
<p>The costs show up in commitments priced for a permanent self. In one of the original studies, people would pay substantially more to see their current favorite band play in ten years than others would pay to see their favorite from ten years ago play next week — buying tickets, in effect, for a fan who will no longer exist. Tattoos, career vows, and &quot;never again&quot; declarations run on the same arithmetic.</p>
<p>The evidence comes with a caveat. The original data compared different people&#39;s memories and forecasts rather than following anyone through time, and when Holly Harris and Michael Busseri did follow people across three waves of a long-running survey, most of them turned out to have predicted too much future change in life satisfaction rather than too little. Quoidbach, Gilbert and Wilson read the same dataset the other way, and the disagreement stands. The practical reading survives the dispute — past change is the best available base rate for future change, and decade-scale irreversible choices deserve to be priced against it.</p>
