Illusion of explanatory depth

We believe we understand how things work — zippers, policies, markets — until we try to explain them step by step.

Use

Useful to test your understanding before you rely on it: explain the mechanism out loud and see where the steps go missing.

The illusion of explanatory depth is our tendency to believe we understand how things work in far more detail than we can actually produce. Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil demonstrated it in 2002 with a simple procedure: people rated how well they understood everyday devices — zippers, flush toilets, cylinder locks — then wrote out a step-by-step explanation, then rated themselves again. The second rating dropped. The explanation attempt didn't teach them anything new; it revealed what was never there.

The engine is a confusion between familiarity and mechanism. We can recognize a zipper, use one, and picture one, and that fluency feels like understanding its interlocking teeth. We also lean, invisibly, on knowledge stored elsewhere — in experts, in manuals, in the device itself working reliably — and mistake access for possession. Nothing flags the difference until an explanation is due, because holding a vague sense of "gears and physics, roughly" and holding the actual causal chain feel identical from inside.

The stakes rise when the object is a policy instead of a toilet. Philip Fernbach and colleagues found that people held confident, extreme positions on policies they could barely sketch the mechanics of — and that asking them to explain how a policy would actually produce its effects, step by step, moderated both the confidence and the extremity. Asking for reasons didn't; reasons are easy to generate. Mechanism is where the illusion fails.

That gives the handle: before relying on an understanding, run the Rozenblit test on yourself. Explain the thing aloud, causally, from input to output, and treat every gap you hit as the actual current state of your knowledge.
