Solomon's paradox

We reason more wisely about other people's problems than about our own.

Use

Useful to understand why we give great advice to others and rationalize our own decisions, and how to borrow the outside view on purpose.

Solomon's paradox is the finding that people reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own. Given a friend's dilemma, we weigh perspectives, admit uncertainty, and look for compromise. Given the same dilemma ourselves, we defend, rationalize, and lose the wide view. The name comes from King Solomon, famous for wise judgments and a chaotic personal life.

Read the restShow less

The practical part is the workaround. In Grossmann and Kross's studies, the gap closed when people examined their own problem from the outside, in third person, as if it belonged to someone else. "What should Pierre do here?" produces measurably wiser reasoning than "what should I do?". The move costs nothing and works precisely where advice from others is hard to accept: it's still your advice.

Explain it differently

  • Plainly

    A friend describes her stalled job search and you see it instantly: the applications are scattershot, the follow-ups too timid, and she's avoiding the one contact who could actually help. Your own search has been stalled for longer, for the same reasons, and none of this has occurred to you. Nothing about her situation is clearer than yours. You're just not inside it.

  • Devil's advocate

    The paradox cuts the other way too: distance isn't automatically wisdom. The colleague who dissects everyone's marriage while his own burns down isn't wise, he's just outside. The outside view works when it's applied to your own case, not when it substitutes for having one. The test of the technique is whether "what would I tell a friend?" ever changes what you actually do.
