Evidence note

Comparative optimism is widely demonstrated, but Harris & Hahn (2011) show that some classic findings can be reproduced by statistical artifacts of rare events and response scales, and the motivational reading remains debated.

Optimism bias

We expect our own future to beat the odds — accidents, delays, and disease are things that happen to other people.

Use

Useful to catch rosy forecasts about your own plans, health, and risks, and to plan from the base rates instead of the best case.

The optimism bias is our tendency to rate our own chances of good outcomes as better than average and our chances of bad ones as worse than average — for other people. Neil Weinstein documented the pattern in 1980: students judged themselves less likely than their classmates to be fired, divorced, or seriously ill, and more likely to own a home and land a good job. Everyone can't be below average risk at once. The math has to break somewhere.

Read the restShow less

The tilt shows up wherever the future is personal. We sign the mortgage with the smooth-career version of the next decade in mind. We skip the travel insurance because food poisoning is a thing that happens in other people's trips. Smokers famously rate their own odds of illness below those of other smokers.

The bias isn't pure error. A forecast tilted toward success makes starting things possible, and Tali Sharot's work treats moderate optimism as a feature of a motivated mind — the same machinery that keeps mood and action running. The evidence has real disputes in it, though: Adam Harris and Ulrike Hahn showed that some of the classic comparative findings can emerge from rare events and rating scales even without any optimism at all.

The practical handle is to keep the optimism and outsource the forecast. For any decision that has to survive being wrong — budgets, timelines, insurance, health screening — start from what usually happens to people in this situation, then argue for why you differ, rather than the other way around.

    Negativity bias

    One tilts the future bright while the other keeps bad news loud in the present, so the same mind can expect things to work out and still flinch at every warning sign.

  • leads toeffect

    Hindsight bias

    Remembering that we saw past outcomes coming makes our record as forecasters look better than it was, and the next prediction gets made with confidence that record never earned.

  • leads toeffect

    Illusion of control

    The more it feels like we are steering an outcome, the better we expect that outcome to be, so an invented sense of control quietly lifts the forecast.

  • specific case ofeffect

    Planning fallacy

    The planning fallacy is optimism aimed at timelines — the same best-case forecasting applied to our own projects.
