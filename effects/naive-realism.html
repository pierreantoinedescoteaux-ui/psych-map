Naïve realism

We experience our take on the world as plain reality, so people who disagree look uninformed, irrational, or biased.

Use

Useful to catch the moment a disagreement turns into a verdict on the other person, and to treat it as a perception gap first.

Naïve realism is the conviction that we see the world exactly as it is — that our perceptions are a plain recording, not an interpretation. Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described its logic as three quiet steps: I see things as they are; other reasonable people, given the same information, will see them the same way; so anyone who persists in disagreeing must be uninformed, not thinking straight, or biased.

Read the restShow less

The trouble starts before any argument does. Perception hides its own work: by the time an event reaches awareness it has already been filtered, weighted, and captioned, and it arrives labeled as fact. There is no inner signal distinguishing "the world" from "my processing of the world," so a difference in interpretation gets experienced as the other person defying reality.

At dinner, a relative makes a remark about your job. You heard a jab; your partner heard an awkward attempt at interest. Neither of you experiences an interpretation — you both experience what happened, and the disagreement afterward is not about the remark anymore. It is about why the other person refuses to admit the obvious.

The workable handle is to treat disagreement as information about differing inputs before treating it as information about the other person. "What are you seeing that I'm not?" is a better opening move than a diagnosis, because on this evidence the diagnosis is usually premature.

    Confirmation bias

    If our position registers as simply how things are, then gathering evidence for it does not feel like taking a side — it feels like noticing what is there.

    Bias blind spot

    Once our own take registers as plain reality rather than a take, the only place distortion can be coming from is the people who see it differently.
