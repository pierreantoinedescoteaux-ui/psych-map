Evidence note

Confident confabulation of reasons replicates across many paradigms; how much genuine introspective access we retain is still debated (e.g., Newell & Shanks, 2014).

Introspection illusion

We treat our reports about why we did things as direct readouts, when they are often stories assembled after the fact.

Use

Useful to trust what you did over what you tell yourself about why, and to check motives against behavior rather than feelings.

The introspection illusion is our confidence that looking inward gives us direct access to the causes of our own thoughts, choices, and feelings — when much of what introspection returns is a reconstruction. Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson made the case in 1977: across studies, people's behavior was moved by factors they never mentioned, while the reasons they did report were often ones the data showed had no effect. They called it telling more than we can know. Emily Pronin later named the illusion itself — not just that access is limited, but that we don't experience the limit.

The mechanism is a gap between process and output. The machinery that weighs options and produces a preference runs outside awareness; consciousness receives the verdict. Asked why, we do what we would do for anyone else — generate a plausible theory — except that when the theory is about ourselves, it arrives feeling like memory rather than guesswork. The same asymmetry feeds our self-assessments: we judge others by what they do, and ourselves by the clean-feeling contents of the inner view.

You pick an apartment and cite the light. The light is real, and so were the rent, the commute, the mood you toured it in, and the fact that it was seen last. Introspection cannot rank those forces; it can only tell a story in which the tellable one did the work.

The honest handle is to weight the outward record. What we repeatedly do under pressure is better evidence about our motives than any account we produce of them, and the account deserves the same polite skepticism we would give anyone else's. How far inward access actually extends is still argued in the literature; the confidence we place in it is the part that reliably overshoots.
