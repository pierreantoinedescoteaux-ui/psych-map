IKEA effect

We value things we built ourselves well above what the same result would be worth from anyone else's hands.

Use

Useful to question whether a project is good or just yours, before effort already spent starts setting the price.

The IKEA effect is our tendency to value things more highly because we made them ourselves — the hours of assembly get folded into the worth of the object. The name comes from flat-pack furniture, but the mechanism doesn't care whether the output is a bookcase, a spreadsheet, a sourdough loaf, or a strategy document.

Read the restShow less

Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely pinned it down in 2012. People who assembled a plain storage box were willing to pay noticeably more for it than people bidding on an identical pre-built one, and amateur origami folders priced their lopsided cranes near what expert work would fetch — while expecting other people to agree. Completion turned out to be the trigger: when builders had to dismantle their creation, or never finished it, the premium evaporated.

The everyday cost isn't overpriced furniture. It's the manager who can't kill the process she designed, the developer defending the tool he wrote over the better off-the-shelf one, the homeowner listing the self-renovated kitchen above what any buyer will pay. Labor bought love, and love is now doing the pricing.

The handle is to separate the two questions the effect merges: how good is this, and how much of me is in it? Getting a valuation from someone who didn't build the thing answers the first question without the second one leaning on the scale.

    Sunk cost fallacy

    Work done with our own hands is worth more to us and also costs more to abandon, so every hour put in both raises what the thing seems to be worth and makes leaving harder.
