Effect

Evidence: mixed — show the evidence note

Evidence note

Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin) found tangible rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation on tasks people already found interesting, while reliably boosting effort on tasks with little inherent interest — the effect depends on task type and reward structure, not a uniform backfire.

Incentivization

Attaching a reward to a task changes how hard we work at it, but not always in the direction you'd expect.

Use

Useful to predict when a reward will boost effort and when it will quietly kill motivation that was already there.

Incentivization is the practice of attaching a reward, or a penalty, to get someone to do something. Offer a cash bonus for hitting a sales target and effort climbs, often exactly as intended. But incentives can also backfire: a volunteer who loved organizing the community garden for its own sake may show up less often once she's paid for it, because the task quietly shifts from something she wanted to do into something she's doing for money. The reward doesn't just add motivation, it can replace the motivation that was already there, and the swap isn't always a good trade.
