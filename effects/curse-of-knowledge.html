Curse of knowledge

We can't unknow what we know, so we explain, teach, and predict as if everyone shares our head start.

Use

Useful to explain things clearly by deliberately rebuilding what it was like not to know them yet.

The curse of knowledge is our inability to reconstruct what not-knowing feels like once we know something — so we explain, teach, and forecast as if our head start were shared. The knowledge doesn't just inform our thinking. It contaminates our model of everyone else's.

Read the restShow less

Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber named the effect in 1989, showing that better-informed traders couldn't set their private information aside even when ignoring it would have made them money. Susan Birch and Paul Bloom later found the same leak in a purer form: adults who knew where an object had been moved couldn't cleanly reason about a person who didn't, especially when the truth felt plausible.

You've been on both ends. The senior engineer explains the deploy process in four sentences, each resting on three things the new hire hasn't met yet, and reads the polite nodding as understanding. Every textbook written by a master, every onboarding doc, every set of driving directions from a local carries the same signature — steps that were once hard-won have become invisible, so they go unsaid.

The curse doesn't yield to trying harder, because the missing experience is precisely the one we can't simulate. The workable handles are external: test explanations on someone who genuinely doesn't know, watch where they stall, and treat every "obviously" in your own draft as a flag for a step you've stopped seeing.
