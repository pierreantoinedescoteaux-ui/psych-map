Anchoring

The first number in the room pulls every later estimate toward it, even when that number is irrelevant.

Use

Useful to catch when a first number is steering your estimate, and to set the anchor yourself when negotiating.

Anchoring is our tendency to lean on the first number we encounter when estimating an unknown quantity. The starting value works like a dropped anchor: we adjust away from it, but rarely far enough, so the final judgment stays tethered to wherever we began.

Read the restShow less

You're buying a used car listed at $14,900. You negotiate hard and drive off at $13,200, feeling sharp. The entire negotiation, though, happened inside a range the seller chose. Had the listing read $12,900, you would have fought your way to a lower number and felt exactly as sharp. The first figure set the field the conversation was played on.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman demonstrated this with a rigged wheel of fortune. Participants watched it land on 10 or 65, then estimated what percentage of United Nations countries were African. A number everyone could see was random still moved the estimates. Anchoring is among the most durable findings in judgment research; it shows up in experts and survives financial incentives, even when people are warned about it.

In depth

Two mechanisms appear to share the work. The first is insufficient adjustment: we start at the anchor and revise until the answer feels plausible, and plausible arrives too early. The second is selective accessibility. Merely considering the anchor recruits anchor-consistent evidence from memory, so when we then estimate, the recruited evidence is already tilted. When a realtor's listing price is high, you notice the renovated kitchen; when it's low, the aging roof.

The effect scales with uncertainty. The less you know about the true value, the more the anchor matters, which is why it hits hardest exactly where we need estimates most: salaries, valuations, sentences, forecasts. Large-scale replication projects have found anchoring effects among the biggest and most reliable in the literature.

Explain it differently

  • Plainly

    Jane needs a bathroom renovated. The first contractor quotes $22,000, which she declines as absurd. The second quotes $16,500, and it feels like a rescue. She signs.

    Her neighbor got nearly identical work done for $11,000. Jane never seriously considered numbers in that region, because her search began at $22,000 and everything after was judged as a distance from it. The absurd quote did its job not by winning but by defining what "reasonable" meant. Jane didn't compare the second quote to the market. She compared it to the first quote, which is a much easier question and the wrong one.

  • At work

    The team is sizing a migration project. Nobody has done one like it before. Alex, thinking out loud, says, "Six weeks? Roughly?"

    The room goes to work on six weeks. One engineer argues it up to eight, citing the legacy integrations. Another concedes seven as a compromise. The estimate that ships is seven and a half weeks, and everyone feels the number was debated rigorously, because it was.

    What nobody debated was the starting point. Six weeks was not an estimate; it was a first guess said aloud. Had Alex mused "four months?", the same rigorous debate would have landed somewhere near fourteen weeks. In estimation meetings, the first number spoken quietly becomes the hypothesis everyone else is amending.

  • Devil's advocate

    An anchor is only a bias when it's arbitrary. When information is scarce, a starting point is not a contaminant; it's the only raw material available. A doctor anchoring on the most common diagnosis, then adjusting for what doesn't fit, is running a reasonable search strategy.

    The naive fix, "just ignore the first number," also doesn't work; the pull operates below deliberate control. What does work is choosing which anchor gets dropped. Making the first offer in a negotiation puts the tether where you want it. Replacing a colleague's blurted guess with a base rate ("our last three migrations averaged eleven weeks") swaps a random anchor for an informative one.
