Self-serving bias

We credit our successes to skill and our failures to bad luck, hard tests, and other people.

Use

Useful to get accurate about what actually caused a result, so wins stay repeatable and losses become fixable.

The self-serving bias is our tendency to explain successes by internal causes — skill, effort, judgment — and failures by external ones — luck, circumstances, other people. The exam we pass measured ability; the exam we fail was badly written.

Read the restShow less

The goal being protected is self-worth. An honest ledger of causes would charge some failures to our own account, and that entry hurts, so the attribution system routes it elsewhere. Miller and Ross opened the modern debate in 1975 by asking whether the pattern even needed a motivational story; a 2004 meta-analysis by Mezulis and colleagues settled the size question, finding one of the largest average effects in social psychology, present in nearly every population sampled — though notably smaller in East Asian samples and in people with depression.

The everyday version runs both directions at once. A quarter with good numbers confirms the new strategy; a quarter with bad numbers gets attributed to the market. Same dashboard, two bookkeeping systems. The cost isn't vanity — it's that miscredited causes make results unrepeatable. If the win was partly luck, next quarter's plan overestimates control; if the loss was partly us, the fixable part goes unfixed.

A workable check is to write the explanation you'd give if the outcome had gone the other way, and see which causes survive both versions.

    The self-compassion break

    The bias exists to protect self-worth after failure; self-compassion supplies that protection directly, making an honest read of what went wrong affordable.

Elsewhere on the map

  • flip side ofeffect

    Fundamental attribution error

    The mirror-image attribution error — we explain others by their character and ourselves by our circumstances; here the split runs along success versus failure.

  • specific case ofeffect

    Cognitive dissonance

    A failure clashes with the belief that we are good at this, and pinning it on bad luck or an unfair test is the cheapest way to make the clash go quiet.
