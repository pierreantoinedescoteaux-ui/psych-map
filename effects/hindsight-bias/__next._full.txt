14:T465,<p>Baruch Fischhoff demonstrated the mechanism in 1975: people given the outcome of a historical episode rated that outcome as more foreseeable than people who didn&#39;t have it, and then underestimated how much the answer had helped them. Neal Roese and Kathleen Vohs later mapped the bias into three stacking layers — misremembering what we predicted, believing the event was inevitable, and believing we personally foresaw it.</p>
<p>You can watch it work after any breakup, layoff, or lost match. The signs were all there, we say — but the signs were assembled afterward, by a memory that now knows which details count as signs. Before the ending arrived, the same details sat in a pile with dozens of others pointing every direction.</p>
<p>The cost is that we grade decisions by their outcomes rather than by the information available when they were made, which makes good process look stupid when it loses and reckless process look brilliant when it wins. The workable counter is a paper trail: write predictions down before outcomes arrive, and audit old decisions against what was on the table at the time.</p>
