Evidence: replicated

Choice blindness

We can miss that our choice was swapped for another — and then fluently defend the pick we never made.

Use

Useful to write down why you chose at the moment of choosing, and to hold your after-the-fact explanations loosely.

Choice blindness is our failure to notice when the outcome of our own choice has been switched — followed by our readiness to explain the substitute as if we had chosen it. Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, and colleagues showed the effect in 2005 with a card trick: people picked the more attractive of two faces, were handed the photo they had rejected, and were asked why they chose it. Most swaps went undetected, and the explanations flowed anyway — sometimes praising features only the rejected face had.

The finding traveled well beyond photographs. In a supermarket, shoppers chose between jams, got a manipulated jar, and defended the flavor they had turned down moments earlier. Similar swaps have worked on the taste of tea, on political survey answers, even on eyewitness identifications. The reasons people give for the un-chosen option are delivered with the same confidence and detail as reasons for real choices.

The mechanism seems to be that we keep no rich record of the choosing itself. The decision process leaves a thin trace; what we hold onto is the outcome in hand. When a "why" question arrives, explanation runs forward from that outcome, not backward from the vanished process — so it works just as smoothly on an outcome that was never ours. From inside, constructing a reason and remembering one feel the same.

Which suggests where the handle is. A reason written down at the moment of the decision — even one line — is a record the later story cannot quietly overwrite. Explanations produced afterward deserve to be held as drafts.

On this map

  • leads toeffect

    Confirmation bias

    Once the swapped outcome is accepted as our choice, reason-finding runs one-way toward supporting it — the same evidence-sorting the bias applies to beliefs.

  • specific case ofeffect

    Introspection illusion

    The swapped-choice experiments are the sharpest version of the after-the-fact story: the explanation arrives fluent and confident even when there was no real choice sitting behind it.
