Evidence note

Field data on investor logins support avoidance after market drops (Karlsson et al., 2009), but a later study of active investors found heightened monitoring after both gains and losses (Gherzi et al., 2014). Information avoidance in general is well documented; when it appears is less settled.

Ostrich effect

We avoid information that might hurt — unopened mail, unchecked balances — as if not knowing kept it from being real.

Use

Useful to recognize dread-driven avoidance of a number or message, and to make checking cheap before the unknown compounds.

The ostrich effect is our tendency to avoid information we expect to be painful — leaving the account unchecked, the envelope unopened, the symptom ungoogled. The information exists either way. What avoidance controls is when we have to feel it.

That's the goal being protected: not the facts, but the mood. Knowing makes a loss psychologically real and forces the reference point to update; not-knowing preserves a fog in which things might still be fine. Galai and Sade borrowed the ostrich for finance in 2006, and Karlsson, Loewenstein, and Seppi found the behavioral signature in 2009: investors logged in to check their portfolios noticeably less after markets fell than after they rose. The evidence has since gotten more complicated — one later study found active investors watching more after drops — but the everyday pattern needs no brokerage account to recognize.

The letter from the tax office sits on the counter for eleven days. Every pass through the kitchen includes a small decision not to open it, and each deferral buys an evening of peace at compound interest, since late fees, missed deadlines, and imagined catastrophes all grow in the dark. Avoided information is one of the few problems that gets objectively worse by being left alone.

The lever is cost, not courage. Checking hurts most as a rare, loaded event; made small and routine — a scheduled glance, a two-minute rule for envelopes — the same news arrives before dread has priced it up.

    Avoided information behaves like any avoided threat: each dodge relieves and reinforces. Approaching in graded steps — open the app, then the statement — breaks the loop.

    Loss aversion

    Because a loss stings more than a gain of the same size pleases, the cheapest relief on offer is not to look at the number at all.
