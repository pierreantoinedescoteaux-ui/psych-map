Sunk cost fallacy

Money and effort already spent keep us committed to things we would never choose fresh today.

Use

Useful to catch yourself continuing something only because you've already put time or money into it.

The sunk cost fallacy is our tendency to continue an endeavor because of what we've already invested in it, rather than what it will return from here. The invested money, time, or effort is sunk: no decision can retrieve it. Rational choice says only future costs and benefits count. We reliably count the past anyway.

Alex paid $400 for a two-day conference. He wakes on day one with a fever, and the honest forecast is eight hours of shivering in a convention center absorbing nothing. He goes, "to get his money's worth." But the $400 is gone whichever way the morning ends; it costs the same to stay in bed as to attend. The only thing still on the table is how he spends a day, and he spends it making himself sicker to honor a payment that can't be honored.

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer documented the pattern in 1985 across experiments and field data, from theater subscribers to hypothetical aircraft projects. The same logic runs through half-finished degrees, over-budget renovations, and wars. The purchase already happened. The fallacy is letting it vote on what happens next.

In depth

In Arkes and Blumer's field experiment, theater season-ticket buyers were randomly given full-price, slightly discounted, or heavily discounted subscriptions. Those who paid full price attended more shows, at least early in the season. Same seats, same plays; only the size of the sunk amount differed, and it predicted behavior it logically shouldn't have touched.

Why does the past get a vote? Partly waste aversion: abandoning feels like converting the investment into confirmed waste, while continuing keeps it provisionally justified. Partly self-justification, since quitting concedes the original decision was wrong. Biologists once assumed evolution would prune this and named the error the Concorde fallacy, after the supersonic jet whose governments kept funding it largely because they already had. The instinct also compounds with time: every additional month invested becomes another reason to invest a month more, which is how mediocre commitments come to consume years.

Explain it differently

  • First person

    I quit a 700-page novel on page 605 once, and I still think about how long it took me to do it. By page 300 I knew the book wasn't working. By page 450 I was reading in a kind of grim administrative mode, ten pages a night, the way you process paperwork.

    The math never favored finishing. The hours I'd spent were spent whether I stopped or not; the only question was whether the next ten evenings went to this book or a better one. But stopping felt like a verdict on the previous month, an admission I had spent thirty hours becoming a person who was wrong.

    What finally freed me was reframing the ledger. Continuing wasn't loyalty to those thirty hours. It was volunteering ten more.

  • At work

    The internal tool is eighteen months old, has consumed two engineer-years, and still isn't stable. A vendor product now covers the use case for $30,000 a year. The build-versus-buy meeting takes an hour, and the phrase that ends it is: "We can't just throw away two years of work."

    Nobody runs the comparison that matters, which is forward-looking and brutally short: from today, finishing the tool costs another engineer-year; the vendor costs $30,000. The two years already spent appear in neither column. They're gone in both futures equally.

    But the two years have a constituency. The engineers who built it are in the room, and killing the project reads as an audit of their judgment. So the sunk cost gets rebranded as "momentum," and the organization spends real future money to protect the accounting of the past.

  • Devil's advocate

    A blanket policy of ignoring sunk costs would make you someone nobody can rely on. Commitments are partly valuable because we honor them past the point of convenience; a friend who re-runs the cost-benefit analysis on your friendship each morning is not a friend. Reputation, marriages, and long projects all depend on a disposition to persist that doesn't recompute constantly, and "that's a sunk cost" can become a sophisticated-sounding license for serial abandonment.

    Sunk costs can also carry information. If you've invested this much, your past self may have seen something real, and eighteen months in a domain buys knowledge a fresh start forfeits.

    The distinction that does the work: honoring a commitment is a claim about the future, that persistence still serves the goal. The fallacy is pure bookkeeping about the past. Ask which one is actually speaking.

    Cognitive dissonance

    Justifying past investment is a dissonance-reduction move — 'I've put in this much, so it must be worth finishing' resolves the clash between effort spent and doubt felt.

  • compounds witheffect

    IKEA effect

    Work done with our own hands is worth more to us and also costs more to abandon, so every hour put in both raises what the thing seems to be worth and makes leaving harder.

  • leads toeffect

    Loss aversion

    Walking away turns the sunk amount into a realized loss, and the urge to avoid booking that loss is much of what keeps us in.

  • leads toeffect

    Planning fallacy

    A project nobody would start at its real price gets started at its imagined one, and by the time the true cost arrives there is already too much spent to walk away.
