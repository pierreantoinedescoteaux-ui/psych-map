Evidence: Empirically Proven

Confirmation bias

We search for, notice, and remember whatever supports what we already believe, and let the rest slide past.

Use

Useful to understand why you keep finding evidence you're right, and to test a belief instead of collecting support for it.

Confirmation bias is our tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information in ways that support what we already believe. It operates at three checkpoints. We ask questions likely to return agreeable answers, we read ambiguous evidence as favorable, and we recall the hits while the misses fade.

None of this feels like bias from the inside. It feels like research.

Alex starts taking a supplement he read good things about. Over the next month he notices several mornings of unusual focus and mentions them to his partner. The foggy mornings get explained by poor sleep, a stressful week, weather. By day thirty he is certain the supplement works, and his certainty is built on real observations. The problem is which observations got kept. Run the same month with no supplement and it contains the same mix of sharp days and dull ones; the belief supplied the sorting.

Peter Wason gave the pattern its name in 1960 after watching people test a hypothesis almost exclusively by looking for evidence that would agree with it. Raymond Nickerson's later review traced the same signature through medicine, law, science, and policy. It may be the most consequential of the reasoning biases, because it compounds: each confirmed belief tilts the next search.

In depth

In Wason's original task, people saw the number sequence 2, 4, 6 and had to discover the rule behind it by proposing their own sequences and hearing yes or no. Most locked onto a guess like "ascending even numbers" and proposed only sequences that fit it, collecting yes after yes. The actual rule was just "any ascending numbers." Discovering that required proposing a sequence you expected to fail, and almost nobody did. The instinct to test by confirming ran the whole show.

Two distinctions matter. First, the bias is mostly a search strategy, not dishonesty; testing where you expect a yes is often efficient, which is why it's so entrenched. Second, it pairs with asymmetric scrutiny: agreeable evidence gets waved through while disagreeable evidence gets audited. That second half explains why more information often fails to change minds. Both sides mine the same data and each finds ore.

Explain it differently

  • In a relationship

    Somewhere in year two, Jane concluded that her brother-in-law is dismissive of her. She can cite the record: the joke about her job at Christmas, the way he checked his phone during her toast.

    The record is real. What it doesn't contain is the afternoon he drove forty minutes to help move her couch, which got filed under "well, family does that," or the times he asked about her work and she gave short answers, reading the question as a setup. Each gathering now runs through the filter, and the filter has never once returned "he was fine tonight."

    Neither of them is lying. But a belief about a person acts like a search engine that only indexes half the interactions, and after enough years the search history looks exactly like proof.

  • At work

    The team shipped a redesign, and the product manager who championed it opens the metrics dashboard. Sign-ups are up 4 percent; session length is down. The write-up leads with sign-ups. Session length gets a caveat about seasonality.

    A skeptical engineer reads the same dashboard and produces the mirror image: the sign-up bump is within normal variance, and the engagement drop is the signal.

    Both are treating one number as data and the other as noise, and each would pass a lie detector. This is why "let the data decide" so often decides nothing. The data arrived after the beliefs did, and ambiguous evidence bends toward whoever is reading it. The fix that actually bites is deciding before launch which metric settles the question, then letting it.

  • Devil's advocate

    A mind without confirmation bias would be expensive to run. You cannot re-derive your worldview every morning; treating each established belief as provisionally true and defending it against noise is what lets you act at all. Most of what you're confident about deserves the tilt.

    The standard fix has a trap in it, too. "Consider the other side" often means marinating in opposing arguments while your scrutiny asymmetry is still switched on, and studies of attitude polarization suggest that can entrench people further; the counterarguments arrive pre-labeled as the weak ones. Exposure isn't the active ingredient.

    Prediction is. A belief that risks nothing is the kind this bias feeds on, so the useful question is rarely "what's the evidence for my view?" It's "what should I see next week if I'm wrong, and would I notice?"
