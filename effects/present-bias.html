Present bias

Rewards available right now feel disproportionately valuable, so tonight keeps winning votes that belong to next year.

Use

Useful to understand why tonight's you overrules tomorrow's plans, and to make decisions while you're still fresh.

Present bias is our tendency to value rewards available right now far beyond what any consistent accounting would justify, while treating all future trade-offs with something like reasonable patience. The distortion sits at the boundary of the present moment, and only there.

Read the restShow less

The signature is preference reversal. Offered $100 in a year or $110 in a year and a week, most of us wait the extra week; the week is cheap from a distance. Offered $100 today or $110 next week, many of us grab the $100, though it is the identical week at the identical price. Nothing changed except which side of "now" the choice sits on.

Daily life runs the same experiment continuously. On Sunday, the Thursday gym session looks obviously worth it, because Sunday's self pays nothing for it. Thursday's self, who holds the actual decision, faces a real couch and an abstract benefit, and the vote goes the way it usually goes. We are patient wholesalers of the future and impulsive retailers of the present, and the retailer is the one at the register.

In depth

Economists call the underlying pattern hyperbolic discounting: the value we assign to a reward falls off steeply over short delays, then flattens over long ones, instead of declining at one steady rate. Richard Thaler documented the reversals in the early 1980s, and David Laibson's quasi-hyperbolic model made the machinery tractable, treating each person as a sequence of selves whose preferences disagree about now.¹ Ted O'Donoghue and Matthew Rabin, who gave present bias its name, added a sharp distinction: sophisticated people who know they will cave behave very differently from naive people who believe next time will be different.²

The framing explains why deadlines, deposits, and lockboxes exist. A present-biased person who anticipates the next self's vote has a reason to strip that self of options: the savings account with a withdrawal penalty is one self outmaneuvering another. Commitment is what patience looks like when it doesn't trust itself.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    The report is due in three weeks, so there is no version of today on which it must be started. Karim opens the file on day two, feels the cold-start friction, and closes it. Something urgent-ish absorbs the afternoon.

    This repeats with small variations for sixteen days. Each individual choice is defensible; today is genuinely busy, and tomorrow genuinely exists. The cost of one more day is nearly zero right up until it is enormous.

    The final 48 hours contain the whole project. Karim works late, cuts the two analyses that made the report worth writing, and ships something fine. The strange part is that he has done this before and will do it again, each time forecasting that this project, unlike the others, will be started early by whoever he is next month.

  • First person

    Sunday night I build the week the way other people build model railways. Monday: gym before work, inbox zero, the difficult phone call. It's a beautiful week. I feel better just laying the track.

    The person who wakes up Monday at 6:10 has never met the person who built the schedule. He inspects the plan with real curiosity, notes that the gym involves being cold and vertical, and files the difficult phone call under Wednesday. He is not lazy. He is simply the only one of us who ever has to do anything, and he has noticed that the planner keeps writing checks against his account.

    By Thursday I'm drafting next week's schedule. It's even better. It has to be; look who's paying.
