Illusion of control

We feel we can steer outcomes that are actually chance, especially when we get to choose, act, or ritualize.

Use

Useful to separate what you can actually influence from what merely feels influenceable, and to spend your effort on the first.

The illusion of control is our tendency to overestimate how much our actions influence outcomes that are substantially or entirely chance. Ellen Langer identified the trigger in 1975: import the props of skill — choice, familiarity, competition, active involvement — into a chance setting, and confidence rises as if the odds had moved. People valued a lottery ticket they had picked themselves far above an identical assigned one, and none of the picking changed a single probability.

The cues do the work. Dice players throw softly for low numbers and hard for high ones. An investor who spent the weekend researching a stock feels the research as reduced risk, though the market never learned about the weekend. A meta-analysis by Paul Presson and Victor Benassi confirmed the effect across dozens of experiments, strongest where the situation is dressed in skill's clothing.

The tell is effort aimed at the uncontrollable: refreshing the tracking page, re-reading the sent email, rehearsing the interview a ninth time in the shower. Some of that is planning. A lot of it is throwing the dice harder.

The handle is a sorting question borrowed from every serious gambler and pilot: which part of this outcome do my actions actually touch? Pour effort into that part — preparation, position sizing, the checklist — and treat the rest as weather. Wanting the outcome doesn't put a steering wheel on it.
