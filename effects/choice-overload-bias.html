Evidence note

Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010) meta-analysis found the mean effect near zero overall, with choice overload appearing only under specific conditions.

Choice Overload

More options should make a choice easier, but past a point they make it harder to choose at all.

Use

Useful to explain why a shorter menu, not a longer one, sometimes gets more people to actually order something.

Choice overload is what happens when adding more options stops helping and starts making a decision harder. A grocery display with six jam flavors sells more jam than one with twenty-four, because the smaller set is easy to compare and the larger one invites second-guessing and decision paralysis. More choice sounds like it should always be better — you can find exactly what you want — but past a certain point, comparing every option costs more effort than most people are willing to spend, and some just walk away having chosen nothing at all.
