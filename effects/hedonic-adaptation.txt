13:T410,<p>Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined the treadmill metaphor in 1971, and Brickman&#39;s 1978 study made it famous: lottery winners reported no more present happiness than controls and took less pleasure in ordinary events, while paralyzed accident victims, though less happy than controls, were far less devastated than observers assumed.¹ The study&#39;s fame outruns its methods; the samples were small and measured at one point in time, so it is better read as a striking illustration than a proof.</p>
<p>Longitudinal work later refined the picture. Tracking thousands of people across decades, Lucas and colleagues found that adaptation is real but frequently incomplete: people drift back toward baseline after marriage, but events like widowhood, divorce, unemployment, and severe disability can shift the baseline itself for years.² We adapt quickly to possessions and one-time changes, slowly or never to some losses, chronic noise, and long commutes. The treadmill exists. It just doesn&#39;t run under everything.</p>
