Prevalence and correlates are documented across dozens of studies, but the label is not a clinical diagnosis and measured rates run from 9% to 82% depending on which questionnaire and cutoff a study uses (Bravata et al., 2020). Imposter syndrome

We file our own accomplishments under luck or timing, so success never registers as evidence of ability.

Use

Useful to treat the fraud feeling as a filing error about your own record rather than a verdict on your ability, and to check the record instead of the feeling.

Imposter syndrome is the experience of holding real accomplishments and being unable to file them as evidence of ability. The work got done, the promotion arrived, the degree is on the wall, and the internal account still reads: luck, timing, a hiring committee that didn't look closely. Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described the pattern in 1978, in high-achieving women whose records were by any outside reading excellent.

The machinery is an attribution asymmetry pointed inward. Success gets explained by anything except ability — the brief was easy, the competition was thin, someone was in a generous mood — while failure goes straight to the account marked me. Because the evidence that would settle the question keeps getting routed away from ability, achievement never compounds into confidence. It compounds into a longer run of getting away with it.

You can watch it in the preparation. Asked to run the client call because you know the account best, you prepare three times past what the call needs, in case this is the one where it shows. The call goes well. You leave relieved rather than competent, and file the result under prepared enough. The over-preparation is what makes the next round survivable, and it is also what keeps the ability from ever being tested in a way that would have to count.

A caution about the name. Researchers call it the impostor phenomenon; it is not a clinical diagnosis, no cutoff separates people who have it from people who don't, and measured prevalence swings from 9 percent to 82 percent depending on which questionnaire a study picks. What the research does support is narrower: the feeling is common, it does not track measured competence, and it travels with perfectionism and fear of failure. That narrower version is still usable, because a claim about evidence, unlike a mood, can be checked against the record.
