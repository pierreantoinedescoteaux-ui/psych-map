7:T60a,<p>The critique is statistical. Score people on a test, ask them to estimate their own standing, and split them into quartiles, and regression to the mean alone guarantees part of the picture: whoever lands in the bottom bucket got some unlucky measurement error, so their self-estimate, being imperfectly correlated with the score, sits closer to average and therefore above it. Add the better-than-average effect, the near-universal tendency to rate ourselves somewhat above the middle, and the iconic crossing lines emerge. Researchers have generated Dunning-Kruger-shaped plots from random numbers; the pattern also appears with simulated test-takers who have no self-awareness to lack.</p>
<p>When Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski tested the hypothesis with methods that don&#39;t bake the artifact in, using IQ scores and self-assessed intelligence, the unskilled were no worse at self-assessment than anyone else; everyone was miscalibrated upward by a similar margin. The correction has drawn its own correction — Avram Hiller argued in 2023 that the key test rests on how the self-ratings were recoded, and Gignac and Zajenkowski replied that the effect still fails to show up — so the dispute is live rather than closed. What stands either way is humbler but real: self-assessment correlates weakly with measured skill across the board. The correction is a good test of the entry&#39;s own subject, since the effect&#39;s fans, this writer included, found it easy to see confirmation everywhere and hard to audit the graph.</p>
