Evidence note

A meta-analysis of 91 studies finds a small effect (d ≈ 0.31) with clear signs of publication bias (Blanken et al., 2015), and several pre-registered replications have come up weak or empty. The pattern likely exists but is smaller and less reliable than the popular version suggests.

Moral licensing

We treat a past good deed as credit that pays for the next indulgence.

Use

Useful to catch 'I've earned this' mid-sentence, and to judge the next choice on its own terms rather than against your virtue balance.

Moral licensing is the tendency for a good deed to make the next questionable one easier — as if virtue accumulated in an account that indulgence can draw against. Benoît Monin and Dale Miller demonstrated the original version in 2001 under the name moral credentials: people who had just established themselves as unprejudiced were subsequently more willing to express views that risked looking prejudiced.

Read the restShow less

The goal being protected is the moral self-image, and the trick is accounting. Judged on its own, skipping the donation or ordering the third drink might register as a small failure. Judged against the week's balance — the gym sessions, the favor for a friend — it registers as a fair withdrawal. The deed doesn't change; the ledger it's posted to does.

You spend Saturday morning helping a friend move, and by evening the recycling goes unsorted with unusual serenity. That's the plausible everyday scale of the thing — and it's worth holding loosely, because the lab record is genuinely wobbly. The meta-analytic effect is small, published studies run larger than unpublished ones, and direct replications have struggled. Licensing is best treated as a real but modest tilt, not a law of moral hydraulics.

The countermove is to close the account: let each choice answer "is this what I want to do?" rather than "have I earned it?" The first question has an answer; the second has a negotiator.
