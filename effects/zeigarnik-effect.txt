17:T457,<p>The finding began, by legend, in a Berlin café in the 1920s, where the psychologist Kurt Lewin&#39;s circle noticed that waiters held complex unpaid orders in memory with ease and forgot them once the bill was settled. Lewin&#39;s student Bluma Zeigarnik took the observation into the lab: participants worked through a series of small tasks, half of which were interrupted, and later recalled the interrupted ones markedly better than the finished ones.¹ Lewin folded the result into his theory of task tension, a goal-linked pressure that persists until discharged by completion.</p>
<p>Replications over the following decades were inconsistent, with some studies finding the recall advantage, others nothing, and a few the reverse. The effect seems to depend on how invested people are and on whether interruption reads as failure, which can motivate forgetting instead. Notably, Masicampo and Baumeister found that intrusions from an unfulfilled goal largely vanish once a person makes a concrete plan for it, before any actual progress.² The loop apparently closes on commitment, not completion.</p>
