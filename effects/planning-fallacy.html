Planning fallacy

We forecast best-case timelines for our own projects even when our track record argues otherwise.

Use

Useful to understand why everything takes longer than planned, and to estimate from your track record instead of your optimism.

The planning fallacy is our tendency to underestimate how long our own projects will take, how much they'll cost, and what can go wrong, even when we know that similar projects have overrun before. The strange part is that second clause. We hold the base rates and the optimism simultaneously; the history just doesn't get invited into the forecast.

Jane is planning a kitchen renovation. She knows, as trivia, that renovations run late. Her brother's took four months. Her own plan says six weeks, because when she pictures the project she pictures the work itself: cabinets out, wiring, drywall, cabinets in. The picture doesn't contain the contractor's other jobs, the backordered counter, the inspection that fails once, because those aren't part of the plan. They're part of reality.

The forecast comes true nowhere and the pattern comes true everywhere. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the fallacy in 1979 and located the cause: we build predictions from the inside, simulating the steps of this particular case, instead of from the outside, asking what usually happens to projects of this kind. The inside view feels rigorous. The outside view is correct.

In depth

The cleanest demonstration comes from Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross in 1994. Honors students predicted when they'd finish their theses: about 34 days, on average. Actual average, about 55. Even estimates made "assuming everything goes as poorly as it possibly could" came in under reality. The worst case people can imagine is still a scenario, still built from the inside, and reality is not obligated to pick from the imagined list.

Two features sharpen the effect. It's strongest for our own projects; observers estimating someone else's timeline are often gloomier and more accurate, because they have no plan to fall in love with, only the person's track record. And it survives experience, since each overrun gets explained by its particulars: the flu, the vendor, the one weird bug. Singular excuses, serial pattern.

The corrective is called reference class forecasting: start from the base rate of similar past projects, then adjust, reluctantly.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    Sprint planning, Tuesday. The payments migration is estimated at two sprints. Alex has been here four years, and he can list what happened to the last three migrations: the auth cutover that took a quarter, the analytics move that met a compliance surprise, the search re-platform nobody discusses.

    He also wrote today's two-sprint estimate, and he believes it. He can see the whole migration in his head, step by step, and the steps sum to four weeks. The estimate isn't lazy; it's a detailed simulation of a world where only the listed tasks exist.

    A new hire asks the naive question: "What did the last one actually take?" Eleven weeks. The room absorbs this, agrees things are different now, and books five weeks as a generous compromise. Week nine, the compliance email arrives.

  • First person

    I told my editor Friday. It was Monday, the piece was three-quarters drafted, and Friday wasn't even ambitious; it was padded. I remember feeling responsible about the padding.

    What I scheduled was the writing. What the week actually contained was a dentist appointment I'd booked months earlier, a source who answered on day three, a section that collapsed when I reread it, and a Thursday lost to a school closure. None of these were surprising, in the sense that my last ten weeks had each contained three or four items from the same catalog. But the forecast was built from the piece, not from my weeks. The piece needed four days. My weeks reliably deliver two.

    I filed the following Wednesday, apologizing, sincerely, for the second time that quarter. The apology was also part of the pattern.

  • Vividly

    The Sydney Opera House was approved in 1958 with a budget of 7 million Australian dollars and a completion date of 1963. It opened in 1973, scaled back from the original design, at a cost of 102 million.

    The overrun is famous because of the building; the shape of it is anonymous and everywhere. Rail lines, stadiums, software rewrites, PhDs: large projects overrun so consistently that researchers who study megaprojects treat on-time, on-budget delivery as the anomaly needing explanation. Each project's planners had a schedule, a contingency buffer, and a sincere belief that the buffer was conservative. Each buffer was consumed by events from outside the plan's imagination.

    A fifteen-year, fourteen-fold miss is not a failure of intelligence or effort. It's what the inside view does when you give it a big enough canvas.
