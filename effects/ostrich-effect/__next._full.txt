18:T4f2,<p>That&#39;s the goal being protected: not the facts, but the mood. Knowing makes a loss psychologically real and forces the reference point to update; not-knowing preserves a fog in which things might still be fine. Galai and Sade borrowed the ostrich for finance in 2006, and Karlsson, Loewenstein, and Seppi found the behavioral signature in 2009: investors logged in to check their portfolios noticeably less after markets fell than after they rose. The evidence has since gotten more complicated — one later study found active investors watching <em>more</em> after drops — but the everyday pattern needs no brokerage account to recognize.</p>
<p>The letter from the tax office sits on the counter for eleven days. Every pass through the kitchen includes a small decision not to open it, and each deferral buys an evening of peace at compound interest, since late fees, missed deadlines, and imagined catastrophes all grow in the dark. Avoided information is one of the few problems that gets objectively worse by being left alone.</p>
<p>The lever is cost, not courage. Checking hurts most as a rare, loaded event; made small and routine — a scheduled glance, a two-minute rule for envelopes — the same news arrives before dread has priced it up.</p>
