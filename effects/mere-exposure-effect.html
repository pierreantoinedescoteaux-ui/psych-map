Mere exposure effect

Simply encountering something again and again makes us like it more, no persuasion required.

Use

Useful to notice when familiar is passing for true or safe, in your choices and in what you believe.

The mere exposure effect is our tendency to like things more simply because we have encountered them before. No argument is made, no reward is attached; repetition alone does the work. The "mere" is the finding.

Read the restShow less

The effect operates below the level of anything we would call a reason. The song that irritated you in June is, by August, your song, and no new information about the song arrived in between. It played at the café, in two other people's cars, over the supermarket speakers, and each pass filed it a little deeper under known. Familiarity was doing quiet accounting the whole time, and liking is how the balance gets reported to you.

The mechanism seems to run on processing ease. A stimulus we have met before is handled more fluently, the fluency feels faintly good, and we attribute the good feeling to the thing itself rather than to our own practiced perception. Advertisers understood the practical version long before the theory existed: much of what a logo buys is the two hundredth encounter.

In depth

Robert Zajonc published the defining studies in 1968. Participants shown nonsense words, Chinese ideographs, and yearbook photographs rated the ones they had seen more often as more pleasant, with liking climbing as exposures accumulated.¹ The frequency-affect link held across stimuli that carried no meaning for the viewers at all, which is what ruled out explanations based on content.

Robert Bornstein's meta-analysis of two decades of research confirmed the effect across more than 200 studies and mapped its boundaries: it is strongest for brief, even subliminal presentations, grows over the first dozen or so exposures, and can plateau or reverse with heavy repetition, which anyone sick of a hit single already knew.² Later work folded the effect into the fluency account above. One caution for reading it into daily life: exposure amplifies an initially neutral or mild reaction, but it does not reliably launder a strongly negative first impression.

Explain it differently

  • At work

    In January, the proposal to fold the two support teams together is received as somewhere between wrong and offensive. It dies in one meeting.

    It returns in March as a slide near the end of a strategy deck, not for decision, just "directional." In May it is a line in the quarterly narrative. By July, people refer to it as "the consolidation plan," with the definite article, and the argument has shifted from whether to when.

    No new evidence arrived between January and July. The idea's content is unchanged; its familiarity is not. Repetition converted it from foreign object to fixture, and fixtures get the benefit of the doubt. Anyone who has watched an organization "come around" to something has usually watched exposure, not persuasion.

  • Vividly

    The first week in the new neighborhood, everything is slightly wrong. The streets bend at unhelpful angles, the bakery's sign is ugly, the church bells are a nuisance at 8 a.m.

    There is no day on which this reverses. There is only October, when you catch yourself defending the ugly sign to a visiting friend, with feeling. The bells have become how morning sounds. The bent streets are now what streets should do, and the grid layout of your old neighborhood seems, in memory, a little sterile.

    The neighborhood never improved. You walked through it four hundred times, and each pass sanded a little friction off the perception, until the place fit your mind the way worn stairs fit feet. What we call growing to love a place is partly that. The place holds still while the liking is installed by repetition.
