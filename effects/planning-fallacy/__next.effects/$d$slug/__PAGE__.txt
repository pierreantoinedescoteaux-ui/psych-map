2:T403,<p>The cleanest demonstration comes from Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross in 1994. Honors students predicted when they&#39;d finish their theses: about 34 days, on average. Actual average, about 55. Even estimates made &quot;assuming everything goes as poorly as it possibly could&quot; came in under reality. The worst case people can imagine is still a scenario, still built from the inside, and reality is not obligated to pick from the imagined list.</p>
<p>Two features sharpen the effect. It&#39;s strongest for our <em>own</em> projects; observers estimating someone else&#39;s timeline are often gloomier and more accurate, because they have no plan to fall in love with, only the person&#39;s track record. And it survives experience, since each overrun gets explained by its particulars: the flu, the vendor, the one weird bug. Singular excuses, serial pattern.</p>
<p>The corrective is called reference class forecasting: start from the base rate of similar past projects, then adjust, reluctantly.</p>
