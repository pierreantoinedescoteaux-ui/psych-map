Motivated reasoning

We reason toward the conclusion we already want, then experience the result as objective thinking.

Use

Useful to catch the moment a conclusion feels attractive before it feels true, and to ask what you'd accept as evidence against it.

Motivated reasoning is reasoning steered by a goal other than accuracy — we work toward the conclusion we prefer, while the process still feels like honest thinking. Ziva Kunda's 1990 review made the key point: we don't simply believe whatever we like. We believe what we can justify, so the machinery builds a justification.

Read the restShow less

The mechanism is a change of standards. For a welcome conclusion, we ask "can I believe this?" and one supporting reason clears the bar. For an unwelcome one, we ask "must I believe this?" and no amount of evidence quite settles it. Both questions run the same reasoning tools; the goal decides how hard they push.

You feel a suspicious mole and find three articles explaining why it's probably nothing. Each article is real. The search that surfaced them, and the standard that let them settle the question, were working for comfort, not accuracy. The same engine serves ego, group loyalty, and avoided costs, which is why several biases on this map are its special cases.

The practical handle is the asymmetry itself. When a conclusion arrives easily and feels like relief, ask what evidence would count against it — and whether you would apply the same scrutiny to the answer you didn't want.
