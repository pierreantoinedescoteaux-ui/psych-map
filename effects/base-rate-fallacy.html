Base rate fallacy

We judge by the vivid details of the case in front of us and ignore how common the thing actually is.

Use

Useful to weigh how often something really happens before a striking description or a positive test result sweeps you along.

The base rate fallacy is our tendency to ignore how common something is — the base rate — when a specific, vivid description points the other way. Given a personality sketch of a quiet, bookish man, we guess librarian over farmer, even in a population where farmers outnumber librarians many times over. The sketch feels like evidence about him. The proportions feel like background noise.

Read the restShow less

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky traced the mechanism in 1973: we predict by resemblance. If the description resembles our image of a librarian, the base rates barely move the estimate, no matter how lopsided they are. Maya Bar-Hillel's later work showed the pattern holding across problem after problem — people don't lack the statistics, they rank them as less relevant than the individuating story.

The expensive version involves tests. Say a screening test catches a rare condition 95 percent of the time, and one person in a thousand has the condition. A positive result feels like 95 percent certainty. Run the numbers and most positives come from the huge pool of healthy people triggering the occasional false alarm — the true chance can sit under 2 percent. The same arithmetic ambushes hiring signals, fraud flags, and security alerts.

The handle is a fixed order of operations: establish how common the thing is before looking at the details of the case, then ask how much the details should really move that starting number. Specifics adjust a base rate; they don't replace it.
