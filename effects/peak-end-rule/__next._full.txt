1a:T4d8,<p>The founding demonstration is almost perverse. Daniel Kahneman and colleagues had people hold a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds, and separately for 90 seconds — the same 60 seconds plus an extra half minute of slightly less cold water. Asked which trial to repeat, most chose the longer one. More total pain, better ending, better memory. Donald Redelmeier and Kahneman then found the same signature in real medical procedures: patients&#39; overall ratings tracked the worst moment and the last minutes, not the length.</p>
<p>The everyday version runs through vacations, dinners, and jobs. A week of easy travel is remembered through the lost luggage on the last day; a mediocre concert is redeemed by a great encore. When you consult your memory to decide whether to go back, you&#39;re consulting the snapshots, not the week.</p>
<p>The effect earns its two-sided reputation in the evidence: the pattern is solid for short, coherent episodes but often fails to appear when researchers test longer or messier real-life experiences. Where it does apply, the handle is straightforward — spend disproportionate care on how things end, and when evaluating a past experience, deliberately ask what the middle was like.</p>
