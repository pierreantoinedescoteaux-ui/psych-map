Evidence note

Miller & Sanjurjo 2018 showed the original studies debunking the 'hot hand' rested on a selection artifact in how streaks were counted; streak-shooting turns out to be partly real.

Hot Hand Fallacy

A player who's been scoring feels 'hot,' so we expect the next shot to land too — often when it won't.

Use

Useful to question whether a streak in sports, trading, or performance is a real pattern or a run your brain is over-reading.

The hot-hand fallacy is the belief that someone on a winning streak is more likely to keep winning — a basketball player who's hit four shots in a row gets treated as "hot," worth feeding the ball to again. For decades this was filed as a pure illusion: streaks in random sequences look meaningful even when they're statistical noise. More recent work complicates that story. Streak-shooting turns out to be partly real in some data, and the original studies that debunked it had a subtle flaw in how they counted streaks. The belief can overshoot the truth, but it isn't purely invented.
