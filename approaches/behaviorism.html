Behaviorism

The school that said psychology should study what people do, not what they report feeling.

Behaviorism is the school that restricted psychology to what can be observed: behavior, and the environmental conditions that shape it. Its core bet is that behavior is learned — through pairings between stimuli (classical conditioning, Pavlov's dogs salivating at the bell) and through consequences (operant conditioning, actions that pay off getting repeated). If behavior is learned, it can be unlearned, and inner life need not be consulted to do it.

That refusal to consult inner life was the point. Reports about thoughts and feelings cannot be checked; behavior can be counted. The wager bought psychology decades of cumulative, replicable results on how learning works — laws of reinforcement, extinction, and generalization that hold from pigeons to people.

The practical descendants are everywhere: exposure-based treatment of phobias, habit-change programs, applied behavior analysis, and the behavioral half of CBT all run on conditioning principles.

In depth

Behaviorism was declared, not discovered. In 1913 John B. Watson published "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It," arguing that psychology should be a natural science whose goal is the prediction and control of behavior. His target was the introspectionist method that then dominated the field — trained observers describing their own conscious experience, in the tradition of Wundt and Titchener — which Watson considered unverifiable. Pavlov's conditioning work supplied a mechanism; B. F. Skinner later extended the program with operant conditioning, showing how consequences sculpt behavior without any appeal to what the organism thinks.

The strict version did not survive the cognitive turn. By the 1960s it was clear that language, memory, and expectation do real explanatory work, and psychology re-admitted the mind. But the learning principles held up — conditioning effects replicate about as well as anything in the field — and behavior therapy never left. It survives as the "B" in CBT.

Where it stands

Time focus

present

What matters is the current contingencies maintaining a behavior, not how the history felt.

Root of the problem

learned associations

Problem behavior was learned through conditioning, and what was learned can be unlearned.

Where expertise sits

practitioner

The analyst observes, measures, and designs the contingencies; the client's introspective report is not the data.

How change happens

conditioning

Change the pairings and the consequences, and the behavior follows.

Structure

high

Explicit protocols, measured baselines, and reinforcement schedules — the method is the structure.

Stance on emotion

byproduct

Emotions are conditioned responses like any other, shaped by the same learning principles.

Evidence base

strong

Classical and operant conditioning are among the most replicated findings in psychology.

    What changed

    Behavior therapy's habits stayed — measure, test, assign homework — but cognition joined behavior as something you could observe and modify. A thought became a hypothesis to check against evidence, not just private noise.
