Cognitive behavioral therapy

The family of approaches that treats thoughts as behavior you can notice, test, and change.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a family of approaches built on one move: treat a thought the way a scientist treats a hypothesis. Distress, on the CBT account, often follows not from events but from systematically distorted appraisals of them — a canceled plan read as rejection, one mistake read as proof of incompetence. Those readings feel like perception. CBT treats them as claims that can be written down and tested against evidence.

The testing is literal. A client who believes "if I speak up, I'll be humiliated" designs, with the therapist, a small experiment: speak up once, record what actually happens, compare it to the prediction. This collaborative empiricism — therapist and client running experiments together — is the working posture of the whole family. Sessions run on agendas and homework, and progress gets measured rather than sensed.

The result is a structured, time-limited therapy focused on the present — and the most heavily trialed one in existence.

In depth

CBT began as a failed replication. In the early 1960s Aaron Beck, then a psychoanalyst, set out to verify the analytic account of depression as hostility turned inward. His data showed something else: depressed patients ran streams of spontaneous negative thoughts — about themselves, the world, and the future — that were biased in consistent, describable ways. Beck published the cognitive model in 1967 and spent the next decade building cognitive therapy around it, borrowing behavior therapy's discipline of measurement and homework while making thought itself the target.

He was not the first to make the cognitive turn — Albert Ellis had been disputing beliefs since 1955 — but Beck's stream arrived with manuals and trial data, and the two converged into the CBT family through the 1970s and 80s. Today CBT has the largest evidence base of any psychotherapy, with strong trial support for depression and the anxiety disorders. The debate that remains is comparative: whether it outperforms other structured, bona fide therapies is still contested.

Where it stands

Time focus

present

The work targets current thoughts and behaviors; history is context, not the site of change.

Root of the problem

distorted appraisals

Distress follows from systematically biased interpretations of events, not from the events alone.

Where expertise sits

shared

Collaborative empiricism — therapist and client design experiments together, and the client becomes their own investigator.

How change happens

testing beliefs

Beliefs are treated as hypotheses to be checked against evidence, in session and in homework.

Structure

high

Sessions run on agendas, homework, and measured outcomes; protocols exist for specific problems.

Stance on emotion

downstream of thought

Emotion is taken seriously, but the route to it runs through the appraisal that produced it.

Evidence base

strong

The most trialed psychotherapy family by a wide margin, though its edge over other bona fide therapies is debated.

Origins

  • ←Evolved fromapproach

    Behaviorism

    CBT kept behavior therapy's experimental stance while making thought a legitimate target of change.

    What changed

    Behavior therapy's habits stayed — measure, test, assign homework — but cognition joined behavior as something you could observe and modify. A thought became a hypothesis to check against evidence, not just private noise.

    Broke away — Aaron Beck

  • ←Evolved fromapproach

    Rational emotive behavior therapy

    REBT made the cognitive turn first; Beck's parallel work and Ellis's stream merged into the modern CBT family.

    What changed

    Not a direct handoff: Beck developed cognitive therapy in parallel from his own depression research in the 1960s. But REBT had demonstrated a decade earlier that disputing present beliefs could be a therapy in itself, and the two streams converged into the CBT family — with Beck's collaborative, evidence-testing style setting the tone.

    The thought record

    Beck's cognitive therapy made the record its central homework: automatic thoughts written down and tested against evidence.

  • →Branched intoapproach

    Acceptance and commitment therapy

    ACT came out of the CBT tradition but dropped its central move — disputing what thoughts say.

    What changed

    The target shifted from the content of thoughts to our relationship with them. Instead of testing or correcting a thought, ACT works on acceptance (making room for unwanted feelings), defusion (seeing thoughts as thoughts rather than facts), and committed action toward chosen values. This is the turn usually called the 'third wave' of behavior therapy.

    Broke away — Steven Hayes
