Acceptance and commitment therapy

The therapy that stopped arguing with thoughts and started changing our relationship to them.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT, said as the word "act") starts from an unusual diagnosis: the problem is not the painful thought or feeling, but the war against it. The struggle to control unwanted inner experience — suppressing it, arguing with it — is what ACT calls experiential avoidance, and it tends to shrink a life faster than the pain itself would.

So ACT declines the war. Instead of testing whether a thought is true, it changes what the thought is held as: defusion means noticing "I'm having the thought that I'll fail" rather than living inside "I'll fail." Instead of reducing a feeling, acceptance means making room for it while doing what matters anyway. What matters is defined by the client's values — chosen directions like being a present parent — and the therapy's endpoint is committed action along them.

The goal is psychological flexibility: carrying difficult inner weather while still steering.

In depth

ACT grew out of behavior analysis, not out of dissatisfaction with it. In the mid-1980s Steven Hayes and colleagues began testing an approach then called comprehensive distancing, built on a behavioral account of how language lets thoughts function as if they were the events they describe. That research program became relational frame theory; the clinical method became ACT, formalized in Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson's 1999 book.

What it reacted against was CBT's signature move. If language itself makes thoughts feel like facts, then disputing a thought's content keeps you fenced in with it — one more round of the argument. ACT's alternative was to change the relationship instead, folding in mindfulness practices that mainstream behavior therapy had kept at arm's length. Hayes later grouped ACT with kindred approaches as behavior therapy's "third wave," after the behavioral and cognitive ones. The trial base has grown quickly across depression, anxiety, and chronic pain, though it remains younger and less deep than CBT's, and skeptics question whether its outcomes differ from good CBT.

Where it stands

Time focus

present-and-future

Contact with the present moment, paired with committed action toward values that point forward.

Root of the problem

experiential avoidance

The struggle to control or escape unwanted inner experience narrows a life more than the experience itself does.

Where expertise sits

shared

The model applies to therapist and client alike — both are language-users caught in the same traps.

How change happens

acceptance and values

Defusion and willingness open space around thoughts and feelings; values-based action fills it.

Structure

moderate

Six named processes and many exercises, but sessions flex around metaphor and experience rather than fixed agendas.

Stance on emotion

accepted

Feelings are made room for rather than reduced; symptom relief is a welcome byproduct, not the goal.

Evidence base

growing

Hundreds of trials across many conditions, but the pile is younger and thinner than CBT's, and many trials are small.

    What changed

    The target shifted from the content of thoughts to our relationship with them. Instead of testing or correcting a thought, ACT works on acceptance (making room for unwanted feelings), defusion (seeing thoughts as thoughts rather than facts), and committed action toward chosen values. This is the turn usually called the 'third wave' of behavior therapy.
