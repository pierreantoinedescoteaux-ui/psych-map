14:T483,<p>ACT grew out of behavior analysis, not out of dissatisfaction with it. In the mid-1980s Steven Hayes and colleagues began testing an approach then called comprehensive distancing, built on a behavioral account of how language lets thoughts function as if they were the events they describe. That research program became relational frame theory; the clinical method became ACT, formalized in Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson&#39;s 1999 book.</p>
<p>What it reacted against was CBT&#39;s signature move. If language itself makes thoughts feel like facts, then disputing a thought&#39;s content keeps you fenced in with it — one more round of the argument. ACT&#39;s alternative was to change the relationship instead, folding in mindfulness practices that mainstream behavior therapy had kept at arm&#39;s length. Hayes later grouped ACT with kindred approaches as behavior therapy&#39;s &quot;third wave,&quot; after the behavioral and cognitive ones. The trial base has grown quickly across depression, anxiety, and chronic pain, though it remains younger and less deep than CBT&#39;s, and skeptics question whether its outcomes differ from good CBT.</p>
