Psychoanalysis

The original talking cure, built on the claim that symptoms mean something — and that the meaning is hidden from the person who has them.

Psychoanalysis treats symptoms as meaningful — the visible trace of conflicts we keep out of our own awareness. Its core bet is that much of mental life is unconscious, and that what stays unconscious keeps its grip on us. A phobia or a slip of the tongue is doing work for some part of the mind that cannot say what it wants directly.

The method follows from the bet. The patient says whatever comes to mind (free association — talking without editing) while the analyst listens for what the material avoids. Dreams get the same reading. So does the relationship with the analyst itself, where old patterns replay in the room (transference). Change comes from insight: naming the buried conflict, then working it through until it loosens.

This makes psychoanalysis a treatment for questions more than complaints (why the same relationship keeps happening, why success brings guilt), pursued over years rather than weeks.

In depth

Psychoanalysis began with a patient. In Studies on Hysteria (1895), Josef Breuer and Sigmund Freud reported that hysterical symptoms faded when patients traced them, under treatment, to memories they could not otherwise reach — one patient named it "the talking cure." Freud pushed the idea further than Breuer would follow: the blocked material was not just forgotten but repressed, pushed out of awareness because it was unacceptable. Against the neurological consensus of the 1890s, which read hysteria as a disorder of the nervous system, Freud made it a disorder of meaning.

Nearly every later school of therapy either descends from psychoanalysis or defined itself against it, and its vocabulary — denial, projection, defense mechanisms — became everyday language. Its scientific standing is another matter. The core claims resist the controlled testing later approaches were designed around, and by modern trial standards the evidence base is thin, though briefer psychodynamic therapies have accumulated respectable trial support.

Where it stands

Time focus

past

Symptoms are traced back to early conflict; the material of treatment is history.

Root of the problem

unconscious conflict

Distress comes from wishes and memories actively kept out of awareness.

Where expertise sits

practitioner

The analyst interprets what the patient cannot see in their own material.

How change happens

insight

Making the unconscious conscious through interpretation and working-through — not skills practice.

Structure

low

Free association follows wherever the patient's material leads; no agenda, no homework.

Stance on emotion

central

Emotion marks the buried conflict; the treatment runs through it, especially in the transference.

Evidence base

contested

Historically foundational, but its core claims were never framed for controlled trials, and modern trial support is thin; brief psychodynamic descendants fare better.

    What changed

    Ellis trained and practiced analytically, then concluded that excavating the past was inefficient — insight into origins wasn't changing his patients. He kept the talking format but moved the target: dispute the irrational beliefs producing distress now, directly and actively.
